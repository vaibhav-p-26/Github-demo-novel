Chapter 1: The Orchard at Dawn

The village of Brackenmere woke slowly, as if it had not yet decided whether the night had truly ended. Thin ribbons of mist drifted between the crooked cottages, curling around fences and stone walls before slipping away into the pale morning light. Above it all, the hills stood watchful and quiet, their slopes covered in grass and thorny briars that seemed to hide more than they revealed.

Elara crossed the lane with her basket tucked against her arm and her dark braid swinging over one shoulder. She was seventeen, quick with her hands, and too curious for her own good. The world beyond her village had always fascinated her—the old roads, the forgotten ruins, the stories told in whispers by the elders. But there was one place the elders never talked about willingly: the orchard at the edge of the Hollow Hill.

It was said the orchard had been planted long before the village existed. It was said the trees sang at night. It was said that anyone who wandered too close would hear their name spoken by the wind. The children were warned to stay away from the gnarled, silver-leaved trees and the black iron gate hidden among them. The adults never spoke of it at all.

Yet on the morning that everything changed, Elara woke with a feeling she could not explain.

Not fear.

Not exactly.

It was more like a pull, deep in her chest, as if something had called to her while she slept.

She dressed in silence, left her mother’s cottage before the kettle began to sing, and walked toward the hill with no plan beyond the strange certainty that she was meant to go.

The air was damp and cool. Dew glittered on the grass like tiny stars. Somewhere beyond the village, a rooster crowed once, then fell still. The silence felt heavy, as though the world itself was listening.

At the foot of the hill, the path narrowed into a muddy trail. Elara hesitated for only a moment before stepping onto it. The orchard was hidden beyond the rise, kept from view by thick hedges and old stone walls. She had never seen it clearly before, only the tips of its twisted branches rising above the trees like skeletal fingers.

Then she heard it.

A soft voice, carried by the wind.

“Elara.”

She froze.

The sound came from nowhere and everywhere at once. It was gentle, almost kind, yet it stirred something deep inside her—something ancient, buried, and awake.

The village behind her seemed a great distance away all at once.

She took a breath and pushed through the hedge.

The orchard spread before her in a hush of silver-green. Trees stood shoulder to shoulder, their trunks knotted and gnarled as if they had been shaped by age and sorrow. Their leaves were pale and almost translucent, catching the morning light in a way that made the whole grove seem lit from inside. There were no birds here. No insects. No sound except the faint creak of branches and the soft whisper of the wind moving through them.

In the center of the orchard, half hidden by ivy, stood a black iron gate.

It was old enough to seem impossible, built without ornament but with such care that each bar looked as though it had been hammered by hand. Vines grew over its frame, and moss clung to its hinges. There was no path leading to it. No sign. No reason for it to be there at all.

Yet Elara knew, with a certainty she did not understand, that she was supposed to approach it.

She moved forward slowly, every step making the air feel colder. The gate was not locked, though it should have been. It stood ajar, just enough for a glimpse beyond it.

Inside, beyond the iron and the ivy, was a narrow passage leading down into the earth.

Not a tunnel, exactly. More like a road that had been buried long before the village was built.

The passage was lined with pale stones, worn smooth by time. It curved out of sight, disappearing into darkness so complete that even the morning light seemed afraid to follow.

And then, from somewhere deep beneath the earth, came a second voice.

Not wind.

Not bird.

A voice with a human shape to it, but softer, older, and full of grief.

“Do not be afraid,” it said.

Elara’s breath caught. Her hands trembled at her sides.

She had never believed in ghosts, or magic, or curses. She had believed in weather, work, and the ordinary rules of the world. But standing there in the orchard, with the strange silver leaves whispering around her and the gate waiting as if it had been expecting her for years, she could not deny that something extraordinary was taking place.

Her mother had once told her, in a rare moment of seriousness, that every family carries one secret they are meant to bury. Elara had laughed then. But now she felt, with startling clarity, that this was the moment she was meant to uncover the one hidden from her all her life.

She reached for the gate.

The metal was cold enough to sting. As soon as her fingers curled around the iron, a pulse ran through it—a deep, humming vibration that traveled up her arm and into her chest. The air around her seemed to tighten, and for a single heartbeat, the entire orchard grew still.

Not silent.

Still.

Like the world had stopped breathing.

Then the wind rose.

It filled the orchard with a sound that was half whisper, half song, and it seemed to carry words in a language Elara could almost understand. The leaves trembled. The branches bowed. Somewhere, very far away, a bell rang once.

And then the gate opened wider, as if it had chosen to admit her.

Elara stepped through.

The path beyond the gate sloped downward, steep and narrow, into darkness that glimmered with a strange silver light. The earth felt alive beneath her boots, warm and pulsing, as if she were walking over the heart of something ancient. The air smelled of rain, cedar, and something sweet and metallic that made her think of old coins and buried treasure.

She kept walking.

Behind her, the orchard disappeared into a fog of pale leaves. Ahead, the passage opened into a chamber deep beneath the hill.

At the center of that chamber stood a stone table, and upon it rested a single lantern made of black glass.

It was burning without flame.

Elara stopped dead.

The lantern was not merely lit; it was alive with a slow, eerie glow. Its light spilled over the stone floor and cast long shadows on the walls, where carvings of thorns, moons, and circles were etched into the rock.

She heard the voice again.

This time it was not a whisper.

It was a command.

“Take it.”

Elara stared at the lantern, heart hammering, and felt the weight of a truth settling over her like a cloak.

She had crossed the threshold. There was no turning back. The orchard had been waiting for her. The gate had opened because she had been called.

And somewhere beneath the hill, beneath the village, beneath all the stories and warnings she had ever heard, something greater than fear was beginning to stir.

She lifted the lantern.

The black glass was cold in her hands.

Then the whole chamber trembled.

And in the dark below, something moved.
